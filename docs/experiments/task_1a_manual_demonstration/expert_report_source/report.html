Executive summary

Additional demonstrations were useful in some cases, but not a reliable improvement.

The experiment asks whether a recurrent model trained on ten similar human demonstrations reaches and holds a target more robustly than the same kind of model trained on one. Across six fixed configurations and two trackers, the median paired benefit of ten demonstrations was negative in six combinations and zero in six; positive in none. Some zero differences reflect universal success, others universal failure.

What this result means. It is evidence against assuming that additional demonstrations improve this fixed learning-and-control pipeline. It is not evidence that human teaching, demonstration diversity, or multi-demonstration learning are generally ineffective.

Exact copies changed none of the 7,800 matched success/failure verdicts. Contractive synthetic variation helped some parent recordings and harmed others. Meanwhile, replay of the recorded trajectories succeeded in 7,750 of 7,800 runs. The recordings were usually followable; learning a state-dependent reference from them was the harder part.

01 / General background

Learning a motion is different from playing it back.

In learning from demonstration, a robot policy is inferred from examples instead of being specified entirely by hand. The examples can be acquired through teleoperation, observation, or direct guidance. The central design questions include what the teacher provides, what the learner observes and predicts, and how performance is tested away from the demonstrations. Argall et al., 2009 survey this framework.

A recording can be replayed on a clock: at each instant, ask the robot to follow the stored posture. A learned state-dependent motion generator instead observes the robot and produces a new reference. This creates an opportunity to react to an unexpected starting posture or disturbance. It also creates a failure mode: once its outputs influence the robot, the learner may encounter states and histories unlike those used for training.

The learner: an echo-state network

An echo-state network (ESN) is a form of reservoir computing. A fixed recurrent network transforms a sequence of inputs into a history-dependent internal state; a comparatively simple output layer is fitted to the desired outputs. Reservoir size, input scale, recurrence, leakage and regularization affect its behavior. This architecture and its practical training choices are described by Lukoševičius, 2012.

Here the ESN uses measured joint position and velocity and predicts the next desired joint position. The readout is trained by ridge regression using demonstrated states. During execution it receives the actual robot state, so its predictions become part of a feedback loop. Low one-step training error alone does not establish that this loop will reach a stable target hold.

The tracker is separate from the ESN. A proportional–derivative controller (PD) corrects position and velocity errors. A computed-torque controller also uses the robot dynamics. Both retain torque limits. The ESN generates a desired motion; it does not directly command unrestricted torque.

Why compare one, ten, copies, synthetic episodes, and replay?

Ten trajectories add human variation. Ten copies add repetition without new human information. Synthetic episodes add deliberately constructed variation without additional human teaching. Replay asks whether the source trajectory can be followed at all under the same tracker and conditions. Together, these comparisons help distinguish data diversity from repetition count and learned behavior from basic trajectory trackability.

02 / Experiment background, aim & scope

A specific test of demonstration count under fixed learning choices.

The broader project studies recurrent reference generators for robot reaching. Its earlier simulation work established the arm, tracking controllers, ESN pipeline, and a set of starting-posture and force disturbances. Planned synthetic trajectories were used before this experiment. The present study replaced that kind of teacher with manually recorded motion and isolated whether adding human demonstrations changes the outcome. No knowledge of those earlier experiments is needed to interpret the comparison here.

Primary question

At the same ESN configuration and tracker, does training on all ten accepted demonstrations improve reaching-and-holding success relative to each single demonstration?

Secondary questions concern exact repetition, contractive synthetic augmentation, and the gap between learned motion and replay. The study freezes six inherited configurations before the manual comparison; it does not optimize them for these manual recordings. Their historical labels are identifiers, not performance rankings in this experiment.

Included

  • One simulated planar reaching task and one target.
  • One operator, ten accepted full-length demonstrations.
  • Six fixed ESN/filter/warm-up configurations.
  • Two fixed trackers, 65 shared evaluation conditions each.
  • Complete successes, failures, aborts and paired comparisons.

Not established

  • Physical robot performance or safety qualification.
  • Generalization to new targets, operators or task types.
  • Performance after tuning each arm independently.
  • A population estimate over random reservoir seeds.
  • A fresh, unseen confirmatory disturbance distribution.

The 65 cases are development conditions inherited from earlier work. They were not chosen to make a particular manual-data arm look good, but they were already known to the research process. Treat the results as a controlled development comparison, not a new held-out benchmark. A separate manual-data parameter-search study is outside this report; none of its results is mixed in.

03 / Methodology

Same task, same comparisons, explicit success criteria.

Robot and task

The simulated arm has two revolute joints in a gravity-free plane, with link lengths 0.30 m and 0.25 m. Every demonstration starts at joint angles [0.2, 1.2] rad. The common endpoint target is [0.10, 0.45] m. Joint positions are bounded by ±3 rad, joint speed by 6 rad/s, and joint torque by 10 and 5 N·m respectively. This is mouse-guided simulated teaching, not physical manipulation of a robot.

Recording, acceptance and preprocessing

The operator recorded trajectories using inverse-kinematics mouse guidance at 50 Hz. Actual timestamps were retained. The most recently saved trajectory was visible as a faint guide, so the recordings are intentionally similar and not independent samples from unrelated operators. Practice was excluded; study take order and display conditions were retained.

The first ten passing takes, from eleven saved attempts in session study-01, became D01–D10. Attempt 3 was rejected for a 0.0612 s sampling gap beyond the frozen 0.06 s limit, and collection continued for the shortfall. Acceptance occurred before model evaluation; weak learned performance did not permit replacing a teacher.

The accepted processed trajectories last 12.87–16.92 s. Smoothing and timestamp-based reconstruction put them on the common 100 Hz grid while preserving the first posture. Natural initial fluctuations, pauses and movement timing remained part of the data. There was no motion-onset cropping, prescribed three-second reach, inter-take time warping, or invented stationary tail. The recordings had to end in a qualifying target hold and pass raw as well as processed validity checks.

Each training episode has equal total loss weight, despite different lengths. In schematic form, the fitted readout minimizes:

Σe=1…E (Nref/Ne) Σt∈e ‖Wze,t − qe,t+1‖² + Eα0‖W‖²F

Here E is the number of episodes, Ne their contributing row counts, Nref a fixed reference count, and z the reservoir features with an explicit bias column. The bias column is scaled together with every other feature and target. Multiplying both data and regularization terms by the copy count leaves the optimum unchanged. This prevents copies from gaining an advantage merely by weakening effective regularization. A separate warm-up primes the model before the full recorded episode enters the loss; recorded pre-roll remains part of the task.

The input transform is frozen from the prior scripted-data pipeline, not fitted separately to each manual take. C10 uses a frozen contractive construction: its perturbation is zero at the initial sample, ramps in over 0.5 s, and tapers through the final dwell. The latent joint-angle noise is a stationary AR(1) process with standard deviation 0.05 rad and coefficient 0.99. Its amplitude is multiplied by the parent’s endpoint-distance ratio to the target, clipped to [0, 1], with exponent 1, and by the start/end boundary envelope. Thus “contractive” describes shrinking perturbations near the target, not a stability proof for the learned system. Each parent supplies nine accepted synthetic episodes within a 36-attempt limit, after derivative reconstruction and validity checks. Different parents have distinct deterministic noise streams. Preserving teacher endpoints and dwell does not guarantee stable learned behavior.

A force pulse is triggered only after the measured arm satisfies the target predicate continuously for 0.5 s. The realized trigger time is recorded separately for each run. A model that never earns the pulse is a failure, not a successful recovery. Force and combined cases must subsequently recover and satisfy final dwell. Each case starts from a fresh reset; an abort does not suppress later cases.

Success is a conjunction, not “the tip came close.”
  • Complete the 30 s active horizon after warm-up.
  • End with at least one uninterrupted second inside the 10 mm target radius, with both joint speeds at most 0.05 rad/s.
  • Keep the generated reference within the position, speed and workspace criteria and satisfy its dwell requirement.
  • Meet the force-trigger/recovery criteria when applicable.
  • Meet the effort criterion: saturation fraction at most 0.005 (0.5%).

The 0.05 rad/s bound is a dwell condition. The much higher 6 rad/s bound is the motion abort limit. A transient may vary greatly without failing; an endpoint inside the circle can still fail because of speed, reference validity, effort or incomplete duration.

Reference velocity and acceleration are computed causally in execution. Replay uses the same estimator policy as its paired configuration; it is not given ideal offline derivatives. Because all six configuration policies differ, there are 60 replay banks, each containing 65 cases × two trackers.

Denominators and units of comparison

There are 31 learned models per configuration: 10 S + 1 M10 + 10 R10 + 10 C10. Across six configurations this gives 186 learned models and 24,180 learned runs. Replay contributes 60 banks and 7,800 runs, for 31,980 runs total. No expected run is missing.

Comparisons are paired by parent recording, configuration, tracker and scenario. The single M10 fit is reused in ten parent comparisons; it is not ten independent fits. A score difference is the number of cases where arm A succeeds minus that number for arm B, over the same cases. Improved/worsened/tied tallies are retained separately. A net zero can hide opposing changes.

All-65 scores weight each scenario once. Consequently the 20-case classes contribute more than nominal or force. We therefore also show class-specific comparisons. Medians and ranges describe the ten observed parents; they are not confidence intervals. Copies, synthetic episodes, overlapping training sets and deterministic evaluations are not independent human observations.

04 / Results

The paired comparison is more informative than the pooled success rate.

The learned runs produce 14,184 successes out of 24,180; replay produces 7,750 out of 7,800. These totals are useful completeness checks but pool different arms, configurations and trackers. They do not answer the one-versus-ten question.

B gives the clearest adverse result: all ten singleton models beat M10 under both trackers. Under PD, M10 solves 14 cases while the singletons solve 59–65. Under computed torque, M10 solves 11 and every singleton solves 41. This is not the effect of choosing one unusually good demonstration as the baseline.

A shows a real benefit for weak parents: with PD, M10 improves D01 by 49 successful cases and D10 by six, ties D03, and loses 15 cases to each of seven other singletons. For D01 specifically, 49 scenarios improve, none worsen, one succeeds under both arms, and 15 fail under both. A rescue exists, but it is not typical of the ten paired effects.

Zero effects need their absolute context: C/PD and E/PD sit at the success ceiling, whereas D is at the failure floor. F's median zero still contains losses against four parents. These are different scientific outcomes despite the same median difference.

The nominal case can hide poor robustness. B/PD M10 succeeds nominally, but only in 5/20 small-offset and 2/20 large-offset cases; every singleton solves all 40 posture-only cases. B under computed torque is different again: singleton success comprises all 41 nominal/posture cases, with none of the 24 force/combined cases successful.

Exact repetition does not help under matched regularization

S and R10 have identical verdicts in all 7,800 matched comparisons (six configurations × ten parents × 65 cases × two trackers). This agrees with the numerical copy-equivalence checks performed before evaluation. It is not a claim of byte-identical trajectories: numerical differences can remain while verdicts and plotted curves coincide.

Synthetic variation is not a dependable substitute for extra teaching

For A/PD, C10 raises D01 from 1 to 59 successes but drops D10 from 44 to six. The D10 net loss of 38 contains three improved and 41 worsened cases. The median C10−R10 effect across parents is zero, spanning −38 to +58. A favorable aggregate can therefore coexist with severe harm to individual teachers.

For B, every C10 model scores zero under both trackers. In C, all singleton, copy and synthetic models solve every case under both trackers. E/PD is also at that ceiling; D stays at zero. There is no universal winner between all ten human recordings and this augmentation recipe: M10's median advantage over C10 is positive only in B, where C10 fails entirely.

Replay separates teacher trackability from learned behavior

Every parent-matched replay solves 63–65 of the 65 cases. B/PD replay solves them all, while M10 solves 14 and C10 none. The gross losses of these learned arms cannot be explained solely by an untrackable source recording. Replay remains a fixed recorded path with feedback tracking, however; it demonstrates neither generalization to another target nor learning a new task.

Replay is not an absolute upper bound. At E/PD, all learned models score 65 while each replay scores 63. That two-case advantage also exists for S, so it does not favor ten demonstrations over one.

06 / Discussion

What explains the result—and what this experiment cannot decide.

Observed: more training variation does not guarantee robust closed-loop motion

The ten teachers meet a common acquisition predicate, and replay usually succeeds. Some fixed configurations also learn excellent singleton behavior. Nevertheless, additional recordings or synthetic transients can worsen the learned reference and its interaction with the tracker. The experiment directly establishes these conditional outcomes; it does not isolate one internal cause.

Hypotheses to distinguish, not conclusions to substitute

Fitting different transient histories may change the readout in a way that improves average demonstrated prediction yet changes its autonomous trajectories or attracting states. Synthetic variations may alter the learned dynamics even when their starts and final holds are preserved. The causal derivative filters and tracking dynamics may amplify or suppress those changes. These mechanisms are plausible interpretations, not experimentally separated explanations.

The ESN has memory. Different desired next positions at superficially similar instantaneous postures are therefore not, by themselves, proof of contradictory targets. A mechanistic study would need controlled ablations—for example, fixing a reservoir while varying teacher subsets, inspecting state trajectories, or comparing training loss with closed-loop behavior. None of those analyses is claimed here.

The tracker and stopping criteria matter

Computed torque is not automatically superior: the same reference generator can show different disturbance outcomes under the two trackers. A force test includes reaching the trigger state and recovering afterwards. A run that never triggers is not evidence of force robustness. A run that aborts has partial telemetry, not an unobserved 30 s continuation. Failure reasons are the deciding criterion recorded by the evaluation; more than one requirement can be violated in the same run.

The 10 mm and 0.05 rad/s final-hold requirements make near misses meaningful. In the C force example, a small residual endpoint error is enough to fail. In B's offset case the failure is much larger. Both count as one failed scenario, so binary robustness scores intentionally discard severity and trajectory quality. The plots restore that context without changing the predeclared verdict.

Limits on the conclusion

  • Small, dependent teacher bank. One operator and ten similar takes, with the previous trail visible, do not cover the diversity of human teaching strategies.
  • One task geometry. There is one start/target task, with bounded perturbations; no new-target or multi-task claim follows.
  • Inherited configurations. Manual-data-specific tuning was not performed. Across the six configurations, several settings and reservoir seeds vary together.
  • Development evaluation. Disturbance conditions were historically known, not freshly held out from the research process.
  • Simulation only. No sensor noise, unmodelled physical contact, actuator uncertainty or hardware qualification is established by these results.
  • Descriptive statistics. Parent medians and ranges are not inferential intervals; the shared M10 model makes its ten paired comparisons dependent.
  • One augmentation construction. Failure of C10 here does not disprove other augmentation methods or tuned perturbation strengths.

Reliability of the evidence

The completed audit rechecked all 31,980 run payloads, recomputed their outcomes and metrics, and rebuilt aggregate evidence. A frozen subset of 300 runs was re-simulated: all 300 reproduced the stored arrays bitwise and had identical verdicts. The issued audit v4 reports 39,562 checks with no failures. A superseded audit that failed its own code-quality gate remains retained. These checks establish reproducibility and consistency with the implemented definitions; they do not independently prove that those definitions are the only appropriate scientific choices.

A storage device failed during the original sweep. The retained data were copied, damaged remnants were quarantined, and the sweep resumed using verified evidence. The final accounting contains every expected run, and the later audit covers the resulting complete store. The interruption is part of the execution record, not a reason to silently omit difficult cases.

07 / Conclusion

Keep the singleton baseline. Test the learned feedback loop.

For this simulated reaching task and these six fixed configurations, ten manual demonstrations did not provide a consistent robustness advantage over one. Exact copies were behaviorally neutral under equivalent regularization. Contractive synthetic variation had substantial, configuration- and parent-dependent effects. Replay's high success rate shows that valid teacher trajectories and robust learned behavior are separate questions.

The practical implication is to evaluate multi-demonstration learning against strong, parent-matched singleton and replay baselines, under both nominal and disturbed conditions. More demonstrations should be treated as a hypothesis about the resulting closed-loop behavior, not as a guaranteed upgrade. The next scientific question is which learning choices make the additional variation useful; that is a separate experiment, not a reinterpretation of the negative result reported here.
